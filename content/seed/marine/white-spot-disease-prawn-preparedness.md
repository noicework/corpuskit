# White Spot Disease Preparedness for Prawn Aquaculture - Farm-Level Guidelines

## Executive summary

These guidelines were developed following a confirmed white spot disease incursion in an eastern
prawn farming region, setting out farm-level surveillance, pond water exchange controls and staff
biosecurity protocols for prawn aquaculture operations. They include an early warning checklist,
distributed to all licensed prawn farms in the affected catchment, and advice on coordinating
biosecurity practice with neighbouring farms sharing water sources, given the disease's
demonstrated capacity to spread rapidly through shared catchment water.

## Background

White spot syndrome virus is highly contagious among crustaceans and can cause near-total pond
mortality within days of clinical signs appearing. The incursion that prompted these guidelines
was first detected in a single pond and traced to water exchange practices common across the
catchment at the time, exposing a gap between individual farm biosecurity practice and the shared
water risk posed by catchment-wide farming density.

## Method

The guidelines were developed by a working group combining epidemiological analysis of the
incursion's spread pattern, consultation with affected and unaffected farms in the catchment, and
transmission risk modelling of alternative water exchange practices. The early warning checklist
was piloted with a subset of catchment farms before wider distribution.

## Findings

- Epidemiological tracing of the incursion identified water exchange during a period of peak
  virus shedding at an infected site as the most probable transmission pathway to neighbouring
  farms, rather than direct stock movement, which was ruled out through movement record review.
- Transmission risk modelling found that restricting pond water exchange during identified high-
  risk periods - following any suspected mortality event at a farm within the same catchment -
  reduced modelled transmission risk to neighbouring farms substantially compared with unrestricted
  exchange practice.
- The early warning checklist, distributed to all licensed prawn farms in the affected catchment,
  prompted farms to report unusual mortality events to a shared catchment coordination point
  within 24 hours, compressing the time between an event occurring and neighbouring farms being
  alerted relative to the incursion response, where several days elapsed before catchment-wide
  awareness.
- Farms piloting the checklist reported that staff biosecurity protocols - footbath use, equipment
  dedication to individual ponds, and visitor movement controls - were inconsistently applied
  before the incursion, and adoption of the checklist's staff protocol section improved reported
  consistency in the pilot group.
- Catchment-level coordination, rather than only individual farm practice, was identified as
  essential given that water exchange risk is inherently a shared-catchment issue that no single
  farm's biosecurity practice alone can fully address.
- No further confirmed cases were reported in the catchment in the two seasons following
  guideline distribution, though the working group notes this cannot be attributed to the
  guidelines alone given the incursion's typical seasonal pattern.

## Discussion

The epidemiological finding that water exchange, not stock movement, was the probable
transmission pathway shifted the guidelines' emphasis toward catchment-level water management
rather than farm-boundary biosecurity alone. This reflects a broader lesson for pond-based
aquaculture in shared catchments: an individual farm's biosecurity standard is only as protective
as the weakest coordination point across the catchment it shares water with.

## Recommendations

1. Restrict pond water exchange during any period following a suspected mortality event at a farm
   within the same catchment, pending confirmation of the cause.
2. Report unusual mortality events to the catchment coordination point within 24 hours using the
   early warning checklist, rather than waiting for confirmed diagnosis before alerting
   neighbouring farms.
3. Apply consistent staff biosecurity protocols - footbaths, dedicated pond equipment, and
   controlled visitor movement - as a baseline practice regardless of current disease status in
   the catchment.
4. Establish or maintain a catchment-level coordination mechanism, given that water exchange risk
   cannot be fully managed through individual farm practice alone.

## Conclusion

These guidelines reframe white spot disease preparedness as a catchment-level coordination
challenge as much as a farm-level biosecurity one, driven by the epidemiological finding that
water exchange during high-risk periods was the probable transmission pathway in the incursion
that prompted their development.

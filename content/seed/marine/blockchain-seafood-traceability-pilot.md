# Blockchain-Enabled Seafood Traceability - Pilot Evaluation

## Executive summary

This review evaluates a blockchain-based traceability pilot tracking premium seafood from vessel
to retail across three supply chains, assessing the data capture burden placed on operators at
each supply chain stage and buyer willingness to pay for verified provenance. The pilot found
strong retail interest, with buyers indicating willingness to pay a premium of up to 8% for
verified provenance, but highlighted at-sea data entry burden as the most cited barrier to full
operator adoption across the participating supply chains.

## Background

Provenance and sustainability claims have become an increasingly important purchasing factor for
premium seafood buyers, but verifying such claims has traditionally relied on paper or spreadsheet
based record-keeping vulnerable to gaps and inconsistency across a multi-stage supply chain.
Blockchain-based traceability has been proposed as a way to create a verifiable, tamper-resistant
record from catch through to retail, though its practical data capture requirements at each stage
had not previously been evaluated in an Australian seafood supply chain context.

## Method

The pilot instrumented three seafood supply chains, spanning wetline and trap-caught product,
with data capture points at vessel landing, processing, transport and retail receival, each
recorded to a shared distributed ledger accessible to supply chain participants and, in
anonymised form, to retail buyers. Operator time burden was logged at each data capture point, and
retail buyers were surveyed on willingness to pay for product carrying verified provenance data
relative to equivalent unverified product.

## Findings

- Retail buyers surveyed indicated a willingness to pay a premium of up to 8% for seafood product
  carrying verified blockchain provenance data, with the premium varying by species and the
  specificity of the provenance claims available.
- At-sea data entry burden was the most cited barrier to full operator adoption among vessel
  operators across all three pilot supply chains, with several operators reporting the process
  interrupted normal catch handling workflow at sea.
- Processing and transport stage data capture was rated as substantially less burdensome than
  at-sea capture by operators at those stages, as it could be integrated into existing barcode
  and weighing processes already in use.
- The pilot successfully tracked product from vessel through to retail across all three supply
  chains without a break in the chain of custody record, demonstrating the underlying traceability
  concept functioned as intended once data was captured.
- Retail buyers accessing the anonymised provenance data most frequently checked catch location
  and catch date fields, with sustainability certification status and vessel identity checked
  less frequently, suggesting buyer interest concentrates on a smaller subset of available
  provenance fields than the full dataset captured.
- Operators using a simplified, pre-populated data entry interface trialled partway through the
  pilot reported meaningfully reduced at-sea data entry time compared with the original interface
  used at the start of the pilot, though it was not tested across all three supply chains.

## Discussion

The pilot demonstrates both the commercial case and the practical adoption challenge for
blockchain seafood traceability: retail willingness to pay is clear and would justify the
investment for many operators, but at-sea data entry burden remains the binding constraint on
adoption. The finding that a simplified interface reduced burden partway through the pilot
suggests the barrier is one of interface design rather than an inherent limitation of the
underlying approach, though this was not conclusively tested across the full pilot.

## Recommendations

1. Prioritise simplifying at-sea data entry interfaces before pursuing wider rollout, given this
   was the most cited adoption barrier and the one improvement trialled produced a meaningful
   reduction in reported burden.
2. Focus provenance data capture and buyer-facing presentation on catch location and catch date,
   the fields buyers most frequently checked, rather than requiring full engagement with every
   captured field to see commercial benefit.
3. Prioritise pilot expansion to higher-value species and supply chains where the identified
   willingness-to-pay premium is likely to most clearly offset the cost of data capture
   infrastructure.
4. Formally trial the simplified at-sea interface across all supply chain types before a full
   rollout decision, given it was only tested partway through this pilot and not across all three
   supply chains.

## Conclusion

The pilot confirms a commercially meaningful buyer willingness to pay for verified seafood
provenance and demonstrates the underlying blockchain traceability concept works end to end, but
full adoption depends on resolving the at-sea data entry burden identified as the primary
constraint by vessel operators.

# Biosecurity Plan for Sea-Cage Finfish Farming - Risk Framework and Response Protocols

## Executive summary

This project developed a tiered biosecurity risk framework for sea-cage finfish operations,
covering stock movement between sites, fallowing regimes between stocking cycles and early
detection protocols for notifiable pathogens. The framework defines four risk tiers based on
stock movement frequency and proximity to other aquaculture sites, and includes response
protocols aligned with national emergency animal disease arrangements alongside a self-assessment
checklist farm operators can use to determine their own risk tier and required practices.

## Background

Sea-cage finfish farming carries inherent biosecurity exposure due to the open-water nature of
sea-cage systems, which cannot be fully isolated from wild fish populations, water-borne
pathogens or vessel traffic in the way land-based aquaculture can. Prior to this project,
biosecurity guidance for the sector existed at a general level without a structured framework
tying specific farm characteristics to a defined set of required practices, leaving individual
operators to interpret general guidance for their own circumstances.

## Method

The framework was developed through a combination of pathogen transmission literature review,
consultation with fourteen sea-cage operators across three states, and trial implementation at
five sites representing a range of stock movement frequency and site density. Fallowing regimes
of varying duration were trialled between stocking cycles at the trial sites, with pathogen
prevalence sampled before restocking to assess carryover.

## Findings

- The framework defines four biosecurity risk tiers based primarily on stock movement frequency
  between sites and proximity to other aquaculture operations, with each tier specifying a
  minimum set of surveillance and stock movement practices.
- Recommended fallowing periods between stocking cycles, calibrated by risk tier, reduced
  pathogen prevalence detected at restocking sampling relative to the shorter fallowing periods
  used in prior practice at the trial sites.
- Sites in the highest risk tier, characterised by frequent stock movement and close proximity to
  other operations, showed the largest pathogen prevalence reduction from adopting the
  recommended fallowing regime, indicating the tiered approach appropriately concentrates the most
  stringent requirements where the underlying risk is greatest.
- Consultation with operators identified stock movement record-keeping as the most commonly
  under-resourced element of existing biosecurity practice, with several operators unable to
  reconstruct a complete movement history for their stock when asked as part of the framework
  development process.
- The self-assessment checklist was trialled with all fourteen consulted operators and correctly
  assigned each to the risk tier independently determined by the project team in twelve of
  fourteen cases, with the two discrepancies both involving operators underestimating their site
  proximity risk.
- Response protocols for notifiable pathogen detection were cross-checked against national
  emergency animal disease response arrangements to confirm farm-level actions did not conflict
  with or duplicate the coordinated response process that would be activated on confirmation.

## Discussion

The tiered structure of the framework is intended to make biosecurity requirements proportionate
to actual risk rather than applying a uniform standard regardless of a farm's stock movement
pattern or site density. The self-assessment checklist's strong but imperfect agreement with
expert tier assignment suggests it is a useful first-pass tool for operators, but that site
proximity risk in particular may benefit from a more structured prompt or, in higher-risk regions,
periodic verification by an external assessor.

## Recommendations

1. Operators should complete the self-assessment checklist to determine their risk tier and
   implement the corresponding minimum surveillance and stock movement practices.
2. Prioritise improving stock movement record-keeping, identified as the most common gap in
   current practice and a prerequisite for effective traceability in any biosecurity incident.
3. Apply the tier-appropriate fallowing period between stocking cycles, given the clear pathogen
   prevalence reduction observed at trial sites, particularly for higher-tier operations.
4. In regions with high site density, consider periodic external verification of self-assessed
   risk tier given the site proximity discrepancies observed in the trial.

## Conclusion

The tiered biosecurity framework provides sea-cage finfish operators with risk-proportionate,
actionable guidance in place of general prior guidance, and trial results support both the
pathogen prevalence benefit of tier-appropriate fallowing and the practical usability of the
self-assessment checklist, with site proximity risk assessment identified as the area most in
need of further support.

# National Recreational Fishing Survey - Participation and Catch Estimates

## Executive summary

This report presents a national survey estimating recreational fishing participation, effort and
catch by species group, intended to inform allocation decisions between recreational and
commercial fishing sectors on a more current evidence base than the previous national survey. An
estimated one in five Australian households included at least one recreational fisher, with
participation rates varying more than twofold between the highest and lowest participating
regions surveyed. Snapper and whiting were identified as the most retained finfish species by
weight nationally, with clear regional variation in the species composition of recreational
catch.

## Background

Recreational fishing represents a substantial share of total fishing effort for a number of
inshore and estuarine species, but unlike commercial catch it is not automatically captured
through logbook or landing records, making periodic survey-based estimation necessary for
allocation and stock assessment purposes. This survey updates estimates from the previous
national survey, conducted using a comparable methodology to allow trend comparison.

## Method

A stratified telephone and diary survey was conducted across all states and territories, with
respondent households recruited through random digit dialling and a subset retained for a twelve-
month catch diary to estimate effort, catch and release rates by species group. Results were
weighted to national population estimates using census data to produce national and state-level
participation and catch estimates.

## Findings

- An estimated one in five Australian households included at least one member who fished
  recreationally during the twelve-month survey period, broadly consistent with the participation
  rate identified in the previous national survey.
- Participation rates varied more than twofold between the highest and lowest participating
  regions surveyed, with coastal and regional areas generally showing higher participation than
  major metropolitan areas.
- Snapper and whiting were the most retained finfish species by weight in the national catch
  estimate, though the ranking of top retained species varied meaningfully by state, reflecting
  differences in regional species availability and angler preference.
- Release rates varied substantially by species, with species subject to minimum size limits or
  targeted primarily for sport rather than table use showing markedly higher release rates than
  species retained primarily for consumption.
- Average annual fishing effort per participating household was higher in regional and coastal
  areas than in metropolitan areas, consistent with the participation rate pattern and suggesting
  the concentration of recreational fishing pressure is geographically uneven relative to
  population distribution.
- Diary-based effort and catch estimates for several inshore species were materially higher than
  estimates that would be derived from boat ramp or access-point survey methods alone, reflecting
  the diary method's capture of shore-based and non-boat-based fishing activity that access-point
  methods can undercount.

## Discussion

The geographic unevenness in both participation rate and catch composition has direct relevance
for allocation decisions that are sometimes made at a state or national level using aggregate
figures: a species heavily targeted recreationally in one region may represent a much smaller
share of recreational catch nationally, and allocation settings calibrated to national aggregates
alone risk being poorly matched to the actual pressure experienced in the regions where a species
is most heavily fished recreationally.

## Recommendations

1. Use regional rather than only national catch and participation estimates when informing
   allocation decisions for species with the pronounced geographic variation identified in this
   survey.
2. Continue diary-based survey methodology in future iterations, given its demonstrated advantage
   in capturing shore-based and non-boat-based effort that access-point methods undercount.
3. Monitor release rate trends by species over time, as they provide a useful complementary
   indicator of recreational fishing pressure beyond retained catch alone.
4. Repeat the national survey on a regular cycle to maintain a current evidence base for
   allocation decisions, given the meaningful reliance on this survey's estimates in the interim
   between national survey rounds.

## Conclusion

This survey provides an updated, methodologically comparable national picture of recreational
fishing participation and catch, and its finding of substantial regional variation in both
supports a regionally informed rather than purely national approach to allocation decisions
between recreational and commercial sectors.

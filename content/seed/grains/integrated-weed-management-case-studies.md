# Integrated Weed Management in Practice - Grower Case Studies

## Executive summary

This review presents four grower case studies of integrated weed management systems combining
harvest weed seed control, crop competition through narrower row spacing and higher seeding
rates, and strategic double-break rotations. Each case study contrasts paddock outcomes on farms
that adopted a full integrated system against neighbouring paddocks on the same property relying
mainly on herbicides. Growers running the full integrated system reported lower herbicide spend
per hectare over three seasons, alongside a slower rate of resistance build-up in weed
populations monitored across the same period.

## Background

As herbicide resistance has broadened across Australian cropping regions, integrated weed
management - combining non-chemical tactics with a reduced and more strategically deployed
herbicide program - has moved from a niche practice to a mainstream recommendation. This review
was commissioned to document real farm outcomes rather than research station trial results, on
the basis that adoption barriers and practical trade-offs are often clearer in a commercial
farming context.

## Method

Four case study farms across South Australia and Victoria were selected on the basis of having
run a full integrated weed management program on at least half the property for three or more
seasons, with a comparable herbicide-reliant paddock available for contrast on the same farm.
Weed seed bank density, herbicide spend and yield were tracked across both management zones for
three seasons.

## Findings

- Harvest weed seed control, implemented through narrow windrow burning or seed impact mills
  depending on the farm, removed the large majority of ryegrass seed set that had escaped in-crop
  herbicide application in the integrated management zones across all four case studies.
- Narrower row spacing combined with higher seeding rates improved early crop competition against
  emerging weeds, measurably reducing weed biomass at the critical early tillering growth stage
  relative to the herbicide-only comparison zones.
- Strategic double-break rotations - two consecutive non-cereal break crops rather than the more
  common single break - produced the largest single reduction in weed seed bank density of any
  individual tactic assessed, though it came with a short-term opportunity cost where break crop
  prices were weaker than cereal prices in a given season.
- Growers running the full integrated system across all four case studies reported lower
  herbicide spend per hectare over the three-season period than the herbicide-reliant comparison
  zones on the same properties.
- Weed seed bank density in the integrated management zones declined steadily across the three
  seasons, while the herbicide-only comparison zones showed a smaller decline in two case studies
  and a slight increase in the other two, consistent with continuing resistance build-up under
  herbicide-only management.
- The main adoption barrier identified across the case studies was not cost but complexity and
  timing - integrated systems require more coordinated decision-making across the whole rotation
  rather than a single in-season herbicide choice, which growers reported as the harder shift to
  make.

## Discussion

The case studies reinforce a finding consistent with broader resistance survey data: herbicide-
only weed management is becoming progressively less effective and more expensive as resistance
broadens, while integrated systems that combine several lower-intensity tactics produce a more
durable outcome. The finding that adoption complexity, rather than cost, was the main barrier
suggests extension effort may be better directed at simplifying the coordination required across
a rotation than at further demonstrating the economic case, which these case studies already
support.

## Recommendations

1. Growers relying primarily on herbicides for weed control, particularly where resistance has
   already been confirmed, should prioritise harvest weed seed control as a first addition given
   its consistent impact across all four case studies.
2. Combine higher seeding rates and narrower row spacing where agronomically appropriate for the
   variety and region, as the early competition benefit compounds with other tactics rather than
   substituting for them.
3. Consider a double-break rotation in paddocks with high weed seed bank density, accepting the
   short-term crop price trade-off against the larger seed bank reduction achieved.
4. Address the coordination and planning burden of integrated systems directly in extension
   material, as this review found it to be the main adoption barrier rather than upfront cost.

## Conclusion

Grower case studies show integrated weed management systems deliver lower herbicide spend and
slower weed seed bank build-up than herbicide-reliant management on directly comparable paddocks,
with the main barrier to wider adoption being the planning complexity of coordinating multiple
tactics rather than their cost.

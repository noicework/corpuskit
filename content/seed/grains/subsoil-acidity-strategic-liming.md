# Managing Subsoil Acidity with Strategic Lime Applications

## Executive summary

This project quantified yield losses attributable to subsoil acidity across New South Wales and
Victorian cropping soils, and trialled deep-banded lime and gypsum-lime blends against
conventional surface application. Deep incorporation lifted subsoil pH within three seasons,
compared with a decade or more typically required for surface liming alone to move acidity
correction below the cultivation layer. In dry finishes, the restored root access to stored
subsoil moisture translated directly into a yield advantage over surface-limed and untreated
comparisons.

## Background

An estimated 50% of agricultural soils in the higher-rainfall cropping zones of south-eastern
Australia carry some degree of subsoil acidity, yet surface liming - the default practice for
decades - corrects topsoil pH far faster than it corrects the acidity constraining root growth
at 20-30cm and below. Where subsoil pH falls below around 4.8 in calcium chloride, aluminium
becomes sufficiently soluble to restrict root elongation, cutting crops off from moisture reserves
that would otherwise buffer a dry spring finish.

## Method

Trial sites were established at nine locations across the NSW and Victorian slopes and Mallee
regions on soils with confirmed subsoil acidity below pH 4.8 at 20-30cm. Treatments compared
untreated controls, surface-applied lime at standard rates, deep-banded lime applied with a
modified ripper at 25cm depth, and a gypsum-lime blend applied by the same method. Soil pH was
measured annually at 0-10cm, 10-20cm and 20-30cm, alongside root density sampling and grain
yield.

## Findings

- Subsoil acidity below pH 4.8 was associated with an average 18% wheat yield penalty across
  affected trial sites relative to matched non-acidic soils in the same paddocks.
- Deep-banded lime lifted subsoil pH at 20-30cm from an average of 4.4 to 5.1 within three
  seasons, compared with negligible movement at the same depth under surface application over
  the same period.
- Root density at 20-30cm increased by 35% under deep-banded treatment relative to untreated
  controls by the third season, coinciding with measurably greater soil moisture extraction from
  that depth during grain fill.
- The gypsum-lime blend produced faster early pH movement than lime alone in the first season,
  but by season three outcomes converged, suggesting gypsum's benefit is mainly one of speed
  rather than final magnitude.
- Return on investment for deep liming averaged 2.3:1 over a seven-year cropping sequence,
  factoring in the higher upfront cost of deep incorporation against the cumulative yield
  response.
- The yield benefit of deep liming was concentrated in seasons with a dry September-October
  finish; in seasons with above-average spring rainfall, the yield gap between treatments
  narrowed substantially.

## Discussion

The central finding is a timing mismatch inherent to surface liming: it corrects the pH growers
can measure easily while leaving the acidity that actually constrains root growth largely
untouched for a decade or more. Deep incorporation addresses this directly but at a materially
higher establishment cost, which the economic analysis suggests is justified primarily on soils
where dry spring finishes are common and stored subsoil moisture is a meaningful part of the
water budget.

## Recommendations

1. Growers on soils with subsoil pH below 4.8 at 20-30cm should consider deep-banded lime rather
   than relying on surface application to correct root-zone constraints within a useful
   timeframe.
2. Where upfront capital is limited, a gypsum-lime blend offers a faster initial response and may
   suit growers prioritising near-term improvement over full-depth correction.
3. Prioritise deep liming investment in paddocks and regions where dry spring finishes are the
   dominant yield-limiting factor, given the concentration of benefit in those conditions.
4. Re-test subsoil pH at 20-30cm every three years following treatment, as the correction
   achieved by deep incorporation in this trial was not permanent and will require re-application
   on a multi-decade cycle.

## Conclusion

Deep-banded lime meaningfully outperforms surface application in correcting subsoil acidity
within a commercially useful timeframe, restoring root access to subsoil moisture and delivering
a positive return on investment on affected soils, particularly where dry finishes are common.

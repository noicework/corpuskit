# Farm Business Succession Planning for Grain Growers - A Practical Guide

## Executive summary

This guide was developed with grain-growing families working through ownership and management
transition across 34 case study farms in NSW, Victoria and South Australia. It covers structuring
conversations between generations, valuing farm assets and off-farm equity fairly, and staging a
transition so the incoming operator builds decision-making capability before taking on full risk.
The review found that fewer than one in three of the case study farms had a documented succession
plan in place at the time of first contact, and that staged transitions consistently produced
better family and business outcomes than a single handover event.

## Background

Succession is widely recognised as one of the highest-risk periods in a farm business's life,
with poorly managed transitions linked to reduced farm productivity, damaged family relationships
and, in some cases, forced asset sales. Grain-growing families face particular pressure because
farm assets are typically illiquid and unevenly distributed between farming and non-farming
children, and because the incoming operator's capability is rarely tested before they assume full
management responsibility.

## Method

Case study farms were recruited through regional grower groups and followed over an 18-month
period through facilitated succession planning conversations. Facilitators recorded the process
used, sticking points encountered, and outcomes at 12 months post-plan. A comparison group of
farms that had completed succession without formal facilitation in the preceding five years
provided a benchmark for typical unstructured outcomes.

## Findings

- Only around one in three family farm businesses in the case study group had a documented
  succession plan before facilitation began, despite most families reporting they had discussed
  succession informally for several years.
- Staged transitions over 5-7 years, in which the incoming operator progressively took on
  budgeting, machinery and marketing decisions before full handover, reported markedly less
  family conflict during the process than the comparison group's single handover events.
- Off-farm equity for non-farming siblings was the most commonly unresolved issue across the case
  studies, present in 26 of the 34 farms, and was the single most common reason cited for delaying
  a plan.
- Farms that used an independent facilitator rather than relying solely on the family's own
  accountant or lawyer reported higher satisfaction with the fairness of the outcome, even where
  the final asset split was materially similar.
- Businesses that separated the succession conversation from the day-to-day operating decisions -
  holding dedicated planning sessions rather than raising succession during routine business
  discussions - progressed through the planning stages faster.
- In the comparison group, unstructured single-event handovers were associated with a higher
  incidence of the incoming operator reporting they felt unprepared for financial decision-making
  in their first full season of sole management.

## Discussion

The consistent finding across the case studies is that succession works better as a staged
capability transfer than a single legal and financial event. Families that began the practical
transfer of decision-making years before the formal ownership transfer produced incoming
operators who were demonstrably more prepared, and reported less conflict, than those where
succession was treated purely as an estate planning exercise concluded at one meeting.

## Recommendations

1. Begin succession conversations well before a transition is financially or logistically
   necessary, using dedicated sessions separate from routine business decisions.
2. Stage the transfer of management responsibility over 5-7 years so the incoming operator builds
   decision-making experience under supervision before assuming full risk.
3. Address off-farm equity for non-farming children explicitly and early, as it was the most
   common source of delay and unresolved tension in the case studies.
4. Engage an independent facilitator rather than relying solely on the family's existing advisers,
   given the association with higher reported fairness outcomes.

## Conclusion

Structured, staged succession planning produces materially better outcomes for grain-growing
families than informal or single-event handovers, both in reported family conflict and in the
incoming operator's readiness to manage the business independently.

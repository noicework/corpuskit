# Controlled Traffic Farming - Ten-Year Economic and Soil Structure Outcomes

## Executive summary

This report presents a ten-year comparison of controlled traffic and conventional traffic
cropping on a Mallee clay soil, tracking compaction, water infiltration and machinery costs from
transition through to full economic maturity of the system. Controlled traffic reduced wheel-track
area from around 30% of the paddock to under 15%, delivered measurable gains in water infiltration
on the permanent traffic lanes, and produced a lower cost of production once machinery was matched
to a common track width and permanent tramlines were established.

## Background

Controlled traffic farming confines all machinery - sowing, spraying and harvest equipment - to
permanent wheel tracks, in principle protecting the cropped area from compaction while
concentrating traffic damage onto a smaller, dedicated footprint. Adoption has historically been
constrained by the capital cost of matching machinery track widths and the multi-year transition
period before soil structure benefits become apparent, making a long-run economic case study
valuable for growers weighing the investment.

## Method

The trial paddock was divided into matched controlled traffic and conventional traffic
management zones on a self-mulching Mallee clay, with all field operations recorded over ten
seasons. Soil bulk density, water infiltration rate and root density were measured annually at
matched positions within and outside wheel tracks. A parallel economic analysis tracked machinery
costs, fuel use and yield across the transition and mature phases of the system.

## Findings

- Wheel-track area fell from about 30% of the paddock footprint under conventional traffic to
  under 15% under controlled traffic once permanent tramlines were fully established by year
  three.
- Water infiltration on permanent traffic lanes improved by roughly 40% relative to randomly
  trafficked areas in the conventional system, attributed to the concentration of compaction
  damage onto a smaller, unused footprint.
- Soil bulk density in the cropped (non-trafficked) zone under controlled traffic declined
  steadily from year one, while bulk density in the conventional system's cropped area showed no
  consistent trend over the same period.
- Machinery matching to a common track width required an upfront capital outlay in year one
  significantly above ongoing annual costs, but this transition cost paid back within six seasons
  through reduced fuel use and lower machinery replacement frequency.
- Average yield in the controlled traffic zone exceeded the conventional zone by a modest but
  consistent margin from year four onward, coinciding with the period when soil structure
  differences between the two systems became most pronounced.
- Fuel use per hectare was lower under controlled traffic in every season from year two, primarily
  due to reduced rolling resistance on the permanent, firmer traffic lanes.

## Discussion

The economic case for controlled traffic in this trial strengthens over time rather than being
immediate: the upfront capital cost of matching machinery is a real barrier, and the soil
structure and yield benefits take several seasons to become measurable. Growers evaluating the
system on a short payback horizon alone may undervalue it, as the largest gains in this trial -
both agronomic and economic - accrued from year four onward once the permanent traffic
infrastructure was fully bedded in.

## Recommendations

1. Growers considering controlled traffic should plan on a multi-year transition and budget the
   machinery-matching cost as a capital investment with a payback horizon of five to six seasons,
   not an immediate return.
2. Prioritise adoption on soils most prone to compaction damage, such as the self-mulching clay
   used in this trial, where the infiltration and structure benefits were most pronounced.
3. Track fuel use as an early indicator of system performance, as it responded within the first
   two seasons well ahead of the slower-developing yield and soil structure benefits.
4. Where capital is constrained, a staged transition - matching the highest-traffic operations
   such as harvest first - may capture a meaningful share of the benefit ahead of full machinery
   matching.

## Conclusion

Controlled traffic farming delivered a durable reduction in trafficked paddock area, measurable
soil structure improvement and a positive economic return over a ten-year horizon on this Mallee
clay soil, but the benefits accrue progressively and should be evaluated over a multi-year
timeframe rather than judged on early-season results alone.

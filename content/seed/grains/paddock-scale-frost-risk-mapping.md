# Paddock-Scale Frost Risk Mapping for Grain Cropping Systems

## Executive summary

Using cold-air drainage modelling combined with ten years of on-farm temperature logging, this
project produced paddock-scale frost risk maps for cropping regions in South Australia and
Victoria. The maps identify low-lying frost traps within paddocks that had previously been
managed as agronomically uniform, supporting variable sowing dates and variety choice matched to
risk zone rather than a single paddock-wide decision. Growers applying zone-matched sowing windows
in trial paddocks reduced frost-affected screenings by an average of 22% relative to uniform
sowing.

## Background

Frost remains one of the most costly and least predictable risks in southern grain cropping,
with radiative frost events driven substantially by topography as cold, dense air drains into
low-lying parts of the landscape overnight. Growers have historically managed frost risk at the
whole-paddock level, choosing a single sowing date and variety maturity to balance frost risk
against the risk of a dry, heat-stressed finish, despite topographic variation within many
paddocks being large enough to matter.

## Method

A network of 40 temperature logging sites was established across cooperating farms in the two
states, positioned to capture a range of slope positions from ridge to valley floor within
individual paddocks. Ten years of overnight minimum temperature data were combined with a
cold-air drainage model built from high-resolution elevation data to produce paddock-scale frost
risk maps. A subset of cooperating growers trialled zone-matched sowing dates and variety maturity
based on the resulting maps against their previous uniform management.

## Findings

- Frost risk varied by up to 3°C within a single paddock, driven by cold-air drainage into
  low-lying points that had previously been treated as no different from the rest of the paddock.
- The temperature logging network recorded frost events at low points in paddocks on nights when
  the nearest official weather station recorded no frost at all, confirming that paddock-scale
  variation is frequently missed by district-level forecasts.
- Zone-matched sowing windows - sowing frost-prone low points to shorter-season varieties or
  delaying sowing in those zones - reduced frost-affected screenings by an average of 22% across
  trial paddocks relative to uniform paddock-wide sowing.
- Ridge and upper-slope positions consistently recorded the fewest frost events across the ten-
  year dataset, supporting these zones as suitable for earlier sowing and longer-season varieties
  without materially increased frost exposure.
- The cold-air drainage model's predicted risk ranking of zones within a paddock matched the
  observed ten-year frost frequency at the logged points closely, indicating the model can be
  used with reasonable confidence to extend mapping to paddocks without direct logging.
- Frost risk maps produced for the ten-year period showed year-to-year consistency in which zones
  within a paddock were most exposed, meaning a single mapping exercise has multi-year value
  rather than needing annual re-assessment.

## Discussion

The core insight is that district-level frost risk information, while useful for broad seasonal
planning, can obscure paddock-scale variation large enough to change sowing decisions. Because
the ranking of risk zones within a paddock proved stable across the ten-year dataset, a one-off
mapping investment can inform sowing decisions for many seasons rather than requiring ongoing
monitoring, making the practice more accessible for growers without existing on-farm temperature
logging.

## Recommendations

1. Growers with paddocks spanning meaningful topographic variation should have frost risk mapped
   at the paddock scale rather than relying on district-level forecasts alone for sowing
   decisions.
2. Match variety maturity and sowing date to identified risk zones within a paddock - shorter
   season or delayed sowing in low-lying frost traps, earlier sowing on ridge and upper-slope
   positions.
3. Treat a single frost risk mapping exercise as having multi-year value given the stability of
   zone rankings observed over the ten-year dataset, rather than reassessing annually.
4. Where direct temperature logging is not available, cold-air drainage modelling from elevation
   data alone provides a reasonably reliable proxy based on the correlation with observed data in
   this project.

## Conclusion

Paddock-scale frost risk mapping reveals meaningful within-paddock variation that district
forecasts miss, and using it to match sowing date and variety choice to risk zone delivered a
material reduction in frost-affected screenings without requiring a change to overall paddock
area sown.

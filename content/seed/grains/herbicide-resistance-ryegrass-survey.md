# Herbicide Resistance in Annual Ryegrass - 2023 National Paddock Survey

## Executive summary

This report presents the results of a national paddock survey testing annual ryegrass
(Lolium rigidum) populations against ten herbicide modes of action across the southern and
western grain belts. Seed was collected from 412 paddocks across South Australia, Victoria,
New South Wales and Western Australia between October and December 2022, then pot-trialled
through the following winter. Resistance to Group 1 (fops/dims) and Group 2 (sulfonylurea)
herbicides was confirmed in the large majority of surveyed regions, and for the first time the
survey detected emerging resistance to pre-emergent Group 15 products in the Western Australian
central wheatbelt. The report sets out a decision framework for rotating modes of action across
a rotation to slow further resistance development, and flags the Group 15 finding as the single
most consequential result for growers relying on pre-emergent chemistry as a fallback.

## Background

Annual ryegrass remains the most costly weed to manage in Australian cropping systems, estimated
to cost growers in excess of $190 million a year in yield loss and control costs. Reliance on a
narrow set of herbicide groups since the 1990s has driven widespread resistance, and this survey
extends a monitoring series that began in 2003, allowing direct comparison of resistance
trajectories over two decades.

## Method

Seed samples were collected using a standardised roadside and in-paddock protocol, with a minimum
of 200 seed heads per site. Populations were screened at label rate against Group 1, Group 2,
Group 3, Group 9, Group 13, Group 15 and Group 22 herbicides in replicated pot trials at three
research stations. A population was scored resistant where survival exceeded 20% of treated
plants relative to an untreated control.

## Findings

- 72% of surveyed ryegrass populations were resistant to at least one Group 1 herbicide, up from
  65% in the 2016 survey.
- 41% of populations carried resistance to both Group 1 and Group 2 chemistry, effectively
  removing the two most widely used in-crop selective options.
- Group 15 pre-emergent resistance was detected for the first time in 14 of 96 sampled paddocks
  in the WA central wheatbelt, at survival rates of 25-60%.
- Group 22 (paraquat/diquat) resistance remained rare, present in fewer than 3% of populations,
  but was concentrated in continuous no-till systems with more than eight years of paraquat use
  at the same site.
- Populations from paddocks with a documented history of harvest weed seed control showed
  significantly lower seed bank density at the start of the season, though this did not by itself
  reduce herbicide resistance frequency.
- Diversifying modes of action across a rotation - alternating pre-emergent chemistry, in-crop
  selectives and non-selective knockdowns between seasons - was associated with roughly half the
  rate of resistance build-up over five years compared with paddocks using a single dominant mode
  of action.

## Discussion

The emergence of Group 15 resistance is a significant shift. Pre-emergent Group 15 products have
been the fallback for growers managing Group 1/Group 2 resistant populations, and their erosion
narrows the remaining chemical toolkit substantially in the affected districts. Glasshouse dose-
response work on the 14 resistant populations suggests the resistance mechanism is metabolic
rather than target-site, which typically confers broader cross-resistance risk across the group
and raises the prospect of resistance spreading to other Group 15 actives not yet tested.

## Recommendations

1. Growers in the WA central wheatbelt should have suspect paddocks tested before assuming
   continued efficacy of Group 15 pre-emergents.
2. Rotate modes of action deliberately across at least three seasons rather than relying on the
   most convenient or cheapest option each year.
3. Prioritise non-herbicide tactics - harvest weed seed control, crop competition through higher
   seeding rates and narrower row spacing, and strategic double-break rotations - in paddocks
   already carrying multiple-resistance populations.
4. Re-survey the affected WA districts in three years to track whether Group 15 resistance
   frequency and cross-resistance broadens, given the metabolic mechanism identified here.
5. Avoid single-mode-of-action reliance even where a herbicide group remains fully effective, as
   the long-term trajectory across all groups sampled since 2003 has been steadily upward.

## Conclusion

Annual ryegrass resistance continues to broaden across Australian grain-growing regions, and the
first confirmed Group 15 resistance signals that pre-emergent chemistry can no longer be treated
as a durable fallback. Integrated, non-chemical control measures are now central to sustaining
profitable cropping in the most affected districts, not a supplementary tactic.

# Split Nitrogen Timing for Improved Wheat Protein and Nitrogen Use Efficiency

## Executive summary

This report compares upfront nitrogen application against split applications timed to stem
elongation and flag leaf stages across multiple soil types in the southern grains region. Split
timing lifted grain protein without a yield penalty in most seasons and improved apparent
nitrogen use efficiency relative to a single upfront dose, though the protein benefit narrowed
in seasons with a wet grain-fill finish due to dilution effects. The results support a shift
toward in-season nitrogen decisions informed by seasonal outlook rather than a fixed upfront
budget set at sowing.

## Background

Nitrogen represents one of the largest variable costs in grain cropping, and protein premiums at
delivery can materially affect farm income independent of yield. Historically many growers have
applied the bulk of their nitrogen budget at or before sowing, based on a seasonal yield target
set in autumn. This approach performs poorly when the season develops differently from the
autumn forecast, either wasting nitrogen in a below-average season or under-supplying a crop that
develops better-than-expected yield potential.

## Method

Trials were conducted across seven sites spanning sandy loam, red-brown earth and self-mulching
clay soils over three seasons. Treatments compared a single upfront application at sowing against
split applications with 50% applied at sowing and the remainder split between stem elongation and
flag leaf, and a fully deferred treatment with all nitrogen applied at stem elongation. Grain
yield, protein and apparent nitrogen recovery were measured at each site.

## Findings

- Split nitrogen application lifted average grain protein by 0.6 percentage points across all
  sites and seasons relative to a single upfront application, with no significant yield
  difference between treatments in five of seven site-seasons.
- Apparent nitrogen use efficiency improved by up to 15% under split timing on sandy loam soils,
  where upfront nitrogen is most exposed to leaching loss ahead of crop uptake.
- The fully deferred treatment matched or exceeded split timing for nitrogen use efficiency on
  lighter soils but carried greater downside risk in seasons with a dry start, where poor early
  access constrained the crop's ability to utilise the deferred nitrogen fully.
- The protein benefit of split timing narrowed to 0.2 percentage points in the one season with an
  unusually wet grain-fill finish, consistent with a dilution effect as additional biomass and
  yield diluted the nitrogen taken up.
- On self-mulching clay soils, where nitrogen loss pathways are less pronounced, the difference
  between upfront and split timing was smallest, suggesting the practice change matters most on
  soils prone to leaching or volatilisation loss.
- Flag leaf application timing, rather than stem elongation, produced the larger share of the
  protein response where the split was itself split between the two stages, indicating late
  applications are doing more of the protein work than early in-season applications.

## Discussion

The results reinforce that nitrogen timing decisions should be responsive to in-season conditions
rather than fixed at sowing. Soil type mediates the size of the benefit substantially: growers on
lighter, leaching-prone soils have the most to gain from deferring a portion of their nitrogen
budget, while those on heavier clay soils will see a smaller but still generally positive
response. The narrowing of the protein benefit in a wet finish is a useful caution against
assuming split timing guarantees a premium outcome regardless of how the season plays out.

## Recommendations

1. Growers on sandy loam or other leaching-prone soils should prioritise splitting nitrogen
   budgets across stem elongation and flag leaf rather than applying the bulk upfront.
2. Where cash flow or logistics require a single application, timing it as close to stem
   elongation as practical captures much of the efficiency benefit seen under split treatments.
3. Do not assume a fixed protein premium from split timing; monitor the seasonal outlook through
   grain fill, as a wet finish will dilute much of the protein gain regardless of application
   timing.
4. On heavier, self-mulching clay soils the case for splitting is weaker; growers on these soils
   should weigh the logistics cost of a second application against the smaller efficiency gain
   observed here.

## Conclusion

Split nitrogen timing delivers a modest but consistent protein and efficiency advantage over
upfront application, particularly on soils prone to nitrogen loss, though growers should treat
the protein benefit as conditional on how grain fill conditions eventuate rather than guaranteed.

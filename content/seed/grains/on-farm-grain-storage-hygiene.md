# On-Farm Grain Storage Hygiene and Insect Detection - Field Guide

## Executive summary

This field guide sets out the pre-harvest cleandown, aeration set-up and sampling routine
demonstrated on working farms in the Wimmera to protect stored grain quality through the storage
season. It covers the fortnightly probe sampling regime growers can run to detect insect activity
before it becomes visible in surface grain, and documents the aeration performance achieved when
systems are correctly set up and maintained. The core message is that hygiene and monitoring,
not insecticide application alone, are the foundation of effective on-farm storage management.

## Background

On-farm grain storage has expanded substantially over the past two decades as growers seek to
capture better marketing opportunities by holding grain rather than delivering directly at
harvest. This shift has increased the consequence of storage failures, as insect infestation or
heating in a large on-farm silo can affect a much greater tonnage and value than the smaller,
shorter-term storage arrangements it has often replaced.

## Method

Demonstration sites were established on four working farms across the Wimmera, each fitted with
temperature monitoring cables and aeration fans instrumented to record run time and airflow.
Standard operating procedures for silo cleandown, aeration scheduling and probe sampling were
implemented and compared against each farm's previous practice over two storage seasons.

## Findings

- A thorough cleandown of silos and augers before filling - removing all residual grain and
  spilled material from the previous season - was consistently identified as the single most
  effective step in preventing carry-over insect infestations, since residual grain is the most
  common source of an active infestation at the start of a new storage season.
- Fortnightly probe sampling at multiple depths within each silo detected insect activity before
  it was visible in surface grain in every case where an infestation subsequently developed,
  giving growers a two-to-three week window to intervene before the infestation became severe.
- Correctly set aeration, run on a controlled schedule tied to ambient humidity and temperature
  rather than run continuously or on a fixed timer, held stored wheat below 15°C through summer
  at the demonstration sites, a temperature at which most storage insect pests breed
  significantly more slowly.
- Two of the four demonstration sites had aeration fans installed but running on a schedule that
  did not match the system design, meaning the grain was not achieving the cooling the equipment
  was capable of; correcting the schedule alone produced a measurable improvement in achieved
  grain temperature without any equipment change.
- Silos without a working seal were unable to be fumigated to a standard sufficient for effective
  insect control when an infestation was detected, turning an otherwise manageable problem into a
  requirement to move or sell the affected grain at a discount.
- Probe sampling combined with pheromone trap monitoring at silo entry points identified insect
  species presence earlier than probe sampling alone in several cases, suggesting the two methods
  are complementary rather than substitutes.

## Discussion

The consistent theme across the demonstration sites is that most storage quality failures trace
back to gaps in routine practice - an incomplete cleandown, an aeration schedule that does not
match the system design, or a silo seal that has degraded unnoticed - rather than to factors
outside the grower's control. Because probe sampling reliably detected insect activity ahead of
visible surface signs, the fortnightly routine functions as an early warning system that converts
a potentially severe infestation into a manageable, low-cost intervention if acted on promptly.

## Recommendations

1. Complete a thorough cleandown of all silos, augers and handling equipment before harvest
   filling begins, as residual grain is the most common source of carry-over infestation.
2. Run fortnightly probe sampling at multiple depths through the storage season, rather than
   relying on visual inspection of surface grain alone, to catch insect activity while it remains
   easily managed.
3. Set aeration schedules to the system's design specification, tied to ambient conditions, and
   verify fan run time against that schedule rather than assuming installed equipment is
   performing as intended.
4. Test silo seal integrity before the season each infestation risk is elevated, since an
   effective seal is a precondition for fumigation being a viable control option if needed.
5. Consider pairing probe sampling with pheromone trap monitoring at silo entry points for
   earlier species-level detection, particularly in larger storage operations.

## Conclusion

Effective on-farm grain storage outcomes in this field guide were driven primarily by routine
hygiene, correctly configured aeration and regular probe sampling rather than by reactive
insecticide use, and growers who addressed these fundamentals avoided the infestations that
affected sites with gaps in one or more of these practices.
